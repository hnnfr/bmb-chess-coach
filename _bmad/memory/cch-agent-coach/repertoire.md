## Hongnam's Repertoire — as Black vs 1.e4

Scope note for this round: both items below are the Black side of 1.e4, since that's where the actual gap showed up (the jawzy14 loss) and where the frequency data pointed. White repertoire and other first moves (1.d4 etc. — rare for his opponents so far) can get the same treatment later if it's useful.

### 1) Shoring up the habitual line — the Philidor (e5 + d6)

**What he actually plays:** e5 in 11/13 Black games, then d6 as the follow-up in 7 of those — landing in Philidor Defense structures (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6) whenever White plays normally. Right now this is a reflex, not a chosen plan — same shape regardless of what White does, confirmed directly by Hongnam at First Breath.

**The plan to give it** (against quiet White setups — 2.Nf3 followed by 3.d4 or 3.Bc4, which is most of what he actually faces): don't rush a central break. Philidor's modern treatment is patient — ...Nf6, ...Nbd7 (not ...Nc6, which blocks the c-pawn and the d6 setup's point), ...Be7, ...O-O, ...c6, and only then look for ...exd4 or a timed ...d5 once the pieces are coordinated to actually back it up. The idea is to make d6 a real structural choice — "I'm keeping the center closed and developing safely" — not just the first square that came to mind.

**Open question:** untested so far whether this patient version actually holds up for him in practice, or whether the passivity itself becomes a new problem (Philidor is solid but can get cramped if Black never finds the right moment to strike back). Revisit once a Philidor game gets analyzed.

### 2) Second weapon — meeting an aggressive White head-on

**The gap this fixes:** the jawzy14 game. White played 2.Nc3 then 3.f4 (Vienna Gambit) instead of a quiet setup, and the e5/d6 reflex had no answer ready — g5 (reactive, weakening) is what came out instead.

**The actual fix, straight from that game's own engine analysis:** against 1.e4 e5 2.Nc3, play **2...Nf6** instead of drifting toward d6, and if White goes aggressive with 3.f4, meet it with **3...d5!** — direct central counter-strike, hitting back at the center instead of either grabbing the f4 pawn (sharp, trappy, rewards the better-prepared side — usually White at this level) or making a non-committal pawn move. This is the exact idea the engine kept recommending all game once things started going wrong, so it's grounded in what actually happened, not handed down from a database.

**The distinction to keep in his head between the two systems:** 2...Nf6 is the trigger that decides which plan he's in. If White stays quiet, he's effectively still choosing Philidor-style setups from there. If White shows aggression (3.f4 or similar), 2...Nf6 is already the right first step toward the sharper, central-counter answer — one move that keeps both doors open a little longer before committing.

**Open question:** only tested in theory, not yet in an actual game — first real Vienna/aggressive-2.Nc3 game that comes up should get flagged immediately as a test of this.
